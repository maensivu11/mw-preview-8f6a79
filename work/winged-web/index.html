the problem

Dating apps turned into a second job. You swipe, you match, and then the real work starts: texting for days, staying interesting, finding a night you're both free, picking a place. Most matches die right there.

The people who drown in that part are often the ones who'd be great on an actual date. Warm in person, bad at the typing. So I built the opposite. You tell Winged who you are and who you're looking for, and your double does the texting and the calling. You just show up.

the apps reward whoever is best at the chat, and that's a different skill from being good company. the people who lose out are warm in person and bad at the typing. i built winged for them.

the system

One dark room, three kinds of light. Every screen sits on pure black, text steps down a fixed brightness ladder, and that calm base is what lets color mean something. Each gradient has exactly one job, so the color tells you whether the machine is talking or a person is.

"Governing rule: cool = the system thinking, warm = humans connecting — never mixed arbitrarily per screen."

the agent experience

An agent that texts strangers for you is a lot of trust to ask for. Most of the design work was earning it, screen by screen. Everything below is the real web app, running in the frame, with sample people and no backend. Every phone on this page is live — tap through it.

draft first. hear it first.

Onboarding, and setting up the profile.

A short voice call trains your double. Then it reads back what it learned about you, on the warm gradient, because this part is about you. Nothing goes public until you accept it, and you hear your own introduction before anyone else does.

someone else speaking for you

Vouching.

The people who know you help your person find you. You invite friends by name, they give two minutes of voice, no app needed, and you approve each vouch before it shows on your profile.

i made approval the step before showing, not a delete button after, because a vouch is words about you that you didn't write.

a vouch is somebody else speaking for you, so it gets the same rule as everything else: it stays yours to approve.
the machine is quieter than the person

Seeing a match, and looking at their profile.

The person’s words sit in a card at reading brightness. The agent’s judgment sits outside it, quieter: the alignment, then the percent in mono caps. The card’s edge and the Introduction pill are warm, because this is a person.

i kept the agent's judgment dimmer than the person's own words, so you read who they are first and the score second.

it sets it up. you take it from there.

The date: before, during, after.

Before: the venue, what to bring up, a card to draw a question from. During: the date chat opens an hour before, so the agent sets it up and the two humans take it from there. After: your double asks how it went, and you can talk it through or leave a note. Switch stages under the phone.

the trust call was where the agent stops. it sets the date up, and the two people take it from there.

Settings, and the wheel.

You can always take the wheel: pause your double, change the places and times, manage your vouches. Tap Settings at the top.

i kept pause one tap away because an agent you can't stop quickly is one you won't let start.

voice introductions

Voice is the cornerstone of everything here. Your double doesn't just text, it speaks, and the introduction it makes is in your own voice.

It starts in onboarding: a short voice call trains your double on how you actually talk. Then it comes back with three voice takes of your introduction. "Pick how your double will introduce you, in your own voice." You play each one back, choose the take that sounds like you, and that becomes the introduction your double carries to matches: the Introduction pill on the match card is this feature arriving on the other side.

And the same draft-then-approve rule holds here. You hear it before anyone else does, and you can change your pick later, on plain black, the same three takes numbered 01 02 03.

the voice picker, live: play a take and watch the waveform · the three takes are synthetic tones, not real voices

stop never reaches the model

The webhook is a gatekeeper before it is a listener.

The agent never sees a message that failed the signature check, or one that said STOP. A sender whose number the carrier stripped (some operators swap in a word) gets a fail-closed path: no reply to an unverified sender, and no link that works as a login.

The phone number is how the system knows who you are: the user is looked up by it, and the sender number is chosen from it.

why this order: anyone can post to a public url, so the signature comes first. and an opt-out is a rule, not a conversation, so it's answered before a model could argue with it.

The agent decides and composes in the same breath.

A deterministic router first picks which of five agents owns you (concierge holding, pre-onboarding, profile setup, active, inactive) from database state, with no model involved. Then that agent makes one call: choose an action, and write the reply as part of choosing it. There is no second pass to drift.

The code, not the model, does the scheduling arithmetic, and the conversation lives in the database: history is loaded, the new turn appended, and the last 40 saved back.

why tool_choice is required: a model allowed to answer in plain text can skip the decision. making the reply an argument of the action ties every message to something the system actually does.

Sending is a short list of refusals, then one call.

Before a text leaves, the code checks a global kill switch, the recipient's opt-out and whether the account was disabled. After it leaves, Twilio reports queued, sending, sent, delivered, or undelivered and failed with an error code, and the status endpoint writes that onto the same log row. It always answers 200 so the provider never retries a receipt.

Two doors out, one number rule. The in-conversation reply calls Twilio directly from the webhook handler; date relays, cron nudges and notifications go through sendSms, which adds the checks, the retries and the receipt. Both pick the sender from the recipient's own number.

why refusals first: the cheapest text to take back is the one that never left. and retries skip errors that retrying can't fix, because resending to a bad number just repeats the mistake.

Winged came out of Antler Nordics and launched in San Francisco and Helsinki.

what it taught me

Give each color a job. Three registers, one meaning each, became how I think about color in any product.

Draft, then approve. "Not yet public until you accept" is the pattern I carried into the next products I built.

Write the system down or it drifts. Winged Web ended with one tokens file that absorbed six drifting greys into one ramp. Next time I'd start with that file.

i'd keep all three. if i can't say what a color is for, it comes out. if something speaks for a person, it comes back as a draft first. and the tokens file comes first, not last.
